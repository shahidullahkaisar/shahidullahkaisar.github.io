What a Z-scan curve actually tells you

10 July 2026 · Research note · 4 min read

If you've seen a Z-scan plot — transmittance on the vertical axis, sample position on the horizontal one, with an S-shaped wiggle in the middle — that wiggle carries a lot of information. It's the measurement at the heart of my thesis, and the idea behind it is surprisingly simple.

The idea in one sentence

You move a sample along the axis of a focused laser beam and watch how much light passes through a small aperture far away. The shape of the resulting curve tells you how the material's refractive index changes with light intensity.

The key relation is n = n₀ + n₂I. The refractive index is not a constant: at high intensity I it shifts by an amount set by the nonlinear refractive index n₂. Z-scan measures n₂ — both its size and its sign.

Peak–valley or valley–peak?

As the sample passes through the focus it experiences different intensities. If n₂ is positive, the sample behaves like a converging lens (self-focusing); if negative, like a diverging one (self-defocusing). At the far-field aperture this appears as a valley followed by a peak, or a peak followed by a valley. The order gives the sign of n₂; the height difference between peak and valley gives its magnitude.

Why black tea?

Polyphenol-rich natural solutions turn out to have measurable, and sometimes surprisingly strong, nonlinear responses. In my thesis I compare three grades of black tea and find a clear ordering in their n₂ values, along with saturation behaviour at higher intensity. That saturation is exactly the property you want for optical power limiting: a material that transmits ordinary light but restricts dangerously intense light.
